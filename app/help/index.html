                      What the health vault is

                      Some details help SkinOracle plan your routine safely: your life stage (like pregnancy or menopause), medications, and how your skin has felt. They’re personal, so they go in a locked vault.

                      • Your devices lock the vault before anything is sent. Our servers only ever hold a scrambled copy.
                      • Only you can open it, with your vault password, your recovery code, or a passkey (Face ID, fingerprint or your device’s screen lock).
                      • We can’t read it, and nobody who gets into our servers can either.

                                Skincare 101

                                What every ingredient does — and what it shouldn't share a night with.

                                Myth or fact

                                You need a 10-step routine

                                Myth

                                Most skin does well with three steps. More steps means more chances for irritation.

                                A routine works because of what is in it, not how long it is. For most people, a gentle cleanser, a moisturizer and sunscreen in the morning cover the essentials.

                                Every extra step adds ingredients your skin has to tolerate. Layering several actives in one night is one of the most common reasons skin starts to feel tight, red or stingy.

                                If you want to add something, add one thing with a clear job — and give it a few weeks before deciding whether it earns its place.

                                Short routines are not a compromise. On a busy night, two minutes done well beats ten minutes done sometimes.

                                Reviewed by: pending dermatologist review

                                General education, not medical advice. If something stings, burns or worries you, pause it and check with a dermatologist or your care team.

                                Myth or fact

                                Natural means gentler

                                Myth

                                Plant-derived ingredients can be lovely — and some are among the most common irritants.

                                "Natural" describes where an ingredient comes from, not how your skin will react to it. Poison ivy is natural too.

                                Essential oils and plant extracts often carry fragrance compounds that sensitive skin may not enjoy, especially when used every day.

                                Many lab-made ingredients, like ceramides or niacinamide, are well studied and very well tolerated.

                                A better question than "is it natural?" is "does my skin feel calm after a few weeks of using it?" Your check-ins can help answer that.

                                Reviewed by: pending dermatologist review

                                General education, not medical advice. If something stings, burns or worries you, pause it and check with a dermatologist or your care team.

                                Myth or fact

                                Oily skin doesn't need moisturizer

                                Myth

                                Oil and water are different things. Oily skin can still feel dehydrated.

                                Oil (sebum) and water content in skin are separate. Skin can feel shiny and tight at the same time.

                                Skipping moisturizer often leaves oily skin feeling worse, especially if you also use drying cleansers or acne actives.

                                A light gel or lotion labeled non-comedogenic is usually enough. You don't need anything heavy.

                                If your skin feels tight after cleansing, that's a sign it could use a little more support, not less.

                                Reviewed by: pending dermatologist review

                                General education, not medical advice. If something stings, burns or worries you, pause it and check with a dermatologist or your care team.

                                Myth or fact

                                SPF is only for sunny days

                                Myth

                                UV light reaches you through clouds, and some of it through windows.

                                A large share of UV light passes through cloud cover, so an overcast day can still add up.

                                UVA, the part linked to long-term changes like dark spots, can also pass through many windows.

                                The simplest habit is sunscreen as the last morning step, every day, whatever the forecast.

                                If you're outdoors for a while, reapplying every couple of hours matters more than the number on the bottle.

                                Reviewed by: pending dermatologist review

                                General education, not medical advice. If something stings, burns or worries you, pause it and check with a dermatologist or your care team.

                                Myth or fact

                                Tingling means it's working

                                Myth

                                A product can work without you feeling anything. Stinging is a signal to slow down.

                                Many effective ingredients do their job quietly. Feeling a sensation is not proof that something is helping.

                                A brief, mild tingle with some acids can be normal for some people. Burning, stinging that lingers, or redness afterwards is your skin asking for a break.

                                If something stings, pause it, keep the rest of your routine gentle for a few nights, and reintroduce it less often if you want to try again.

                                If a reaction is strong, spreading or doesn't settle, stop the product and check with a dermatologist or pharmacist.

                                Reviewed by: pending dermatologist review

                                General education, not medical advice. If something stings, burns or worries you, pause it and check with a dermatologist or your care team.

                                Myth or fact

                                You can shrink your pores

                                Myth

                                Pore size is mostly set by genetics and age. Pores can look smaller, though.

                                Pores don't open and close like doors, and no product permanently changes their size.

                                What you can change is how visible they are. Pores filled with oil and debris look larger; clearer pores look smaller.

                                Regular gentle cleansing, a leave-on exfoliant a few nights a week, and daily sunscreen can all help pores look less noticeable over time.

                                Cold water, scrubs and harsh toners won't shrink them — and scrubbing can leave skin irritated.

                                Reviewed by: pending dermatologist review

                                General education, not medical advice. If something stings, burns or worries you, pause it and check with a dermatologist or your care team.

                                Myth or fact

                                Drinking more water fixes dry skin

                                It depends

                                Staying hydrated is good for you, but dry skin is mostly about the outer barrier.

                                If you're genuinely dehydrated, drinking enough water helps your whole body, skin included.

                                For most people who already drink enough, extra water doesn't noticeably change dry or flaky skin.

                                Dry skin usually improves more from the outside: a gentler cleanser, lukewarm instead of hot water, and a moisturizer applied while skin is still slightly damp.

                                Low humidity, flights and cold wind often leave skin feeling drier. SkinOracle adjusts your routine on those days.

                                Reviewed by: pending dermatologist review

                                General education, not medical advice. If something stings, burns or worries you, pause it and check with a dermatologist or your care team.

                                Myth or fact

                                A higher percentage is always better

                                Myth

                                Stronger isn't the same as better. The right strength is the one your skin tolerates.

                                Many ingredients have a sweet spot. Going higher often adds irritation faster than it adds results.

                                Lower strengths used consistently tend to do more than high strengths used on and off because they sting.

                                If you're new to an active like a retinoid or an acid, start low and slow — a couple of nights a week — and build up only if your skin stays calm.

                                The formula as a whole matters too: two products with the same percentage can feel very different.

                                Reviewed by: pending dermatologist review

                                General education, not medical advice. If something stings, burns or worries you, pause it and check with a dermatologist or your care team.

                                Myth or fact

                                Popping a pimple makes it go away faster

                                Myth

                                Squeezing usually pushes things deeper and can leave a mark that lasts longer than the spot.

                                Squeezing can push inflammation deeper into the skin and raise the chance of a lingering dark mark or scar.

                                A hydrocolloid patch is a calmer option: it protects the spot and makes picking harder.

                                Keep the rest of the routine gentle while a spot settles, rather than adding several drying products at once.

                                Deep, painful or frequent breakouts are worth talking through with a dermatologist.

                                Reviewed by: pending dermatologist review

                                General education, not medical advice. If something stings, burns or worries you, pause it and check with a dermatologist or your care team.

                                Life stages

                                Skincare during pregnancy

                                Keep it simple and gentle, and check anything you're unsure about with your care team.

                                Pregnancy can change skin in lots of ways — more oil for some, more dryness or sensitivity for others, and sometimes darker patches.

                                Retinoids are commonly paused during pregnancy and while trying to conceive. SkinOracle leaves them out of your routine when you tell us you're pregnant.

                                A gentle cleanser, a fragrance-free moisturizer and a daily sunscreen are a solid, low-fuss base. Many people find mineral sunscreens comfortable during this time, and any broad-spectrum SPF you'll wear every day is a good choice.

                                Before starting anything new — especially acne or dark-spot products — ask your OB, midwife or pharmacist. They know your situation best.

                                Reviewed by: pending dermatologist review

                                General education, not medical advice. If something stings, burns or worries you, pause it and check with a dermatologist or your care team.

                                Life stages

                                Perimenopause and menopause

                                Skin often gets drier and more reactive. Comfort comes first.

                                As hormone levels shift, many people notice drier, thinner-feeling or more easily irritated skin. Some notice breakouts again too.

                                A creamier cleanser, or cleansing only at night, can help skin hold on to moisture.

                                A richer moisturizer with ingredients like ceramides or glycerin often feels more comfortable, especially in winter or after flights.

                                If you use a retinoid or acids, it's fine to use them less often. Daily sunscreen stays important.

                                If you're on hormone therapy or have questions about changes you're seeing, your doctor is a great person to ask.

                                Reviewed by: pending dermatologist review

                                General education, not medical advice. If something stings, burns or worries you, pause it and check with a dermatologist or your care team.

                                Life stages

                                Testosterone therapy and facial hair

                                More oil, new breakouts and shaving irritation are common. A few small habits help.

                                Starting testosterone often brings oilier skin and breakouts, especially in the first year. That's a common experience, not a failure of your routine.

                                A gentle cleanser twice a day and a light, non-comedogenic moisturizer are a good foundation. Leave-on salicylic acid a few nights a week can help with clogged pores.

                                If you're shaving new facial hair: use a sharp single-blade or electric razor, shave in the direction of growth, don't pull your skin tight, and finish with a fragrance-free balm. If bumps keep coming back or leave marks, see a dermatologist.

                                If breakouts are painful or leave marks, talk with your care team — there are options they can help you weigh.

                                Reviewed by: pending dermatologist review

                                General education, not medical advice. If something stings, burns or worries you, pause it and check with a dermatologist or your care team.

                                Life stages

                                Teen skin basics

                                Young skin needs very little. Three simple steps go a long way.

                                Teen skin doesn't need products aimed at aging. It needs to be cleaned gently, kept comfortable and protected from the sun.

                                Morning: rinse or gentle cleanse, a light moisturizer, and sunscreen. Evening: gentle cleanse and moisturizer. That's it.

                                Breakouts are very common in the teen years. Scrubbing harder or using lots of products at once usually makes skin more irritated, not clearer.

                                If breakouts are painful, leave marks or affect how you feel, a parent, school nurse or doctor can help you find a plan.

                                Reviewed by: pending dermatologist review

                                General education, not medical advice. If something stings, burns or worries you, pause it and check with a dermatologist or your care team.

                                Life stages

                                Skin during cancer treatment

                                Cancer treatment can make skin drier and more sensitive. Gentle and simple is kind — and your care team comes first.

                                Some kinds of cancer treatment can leave skin drier, itchier or more sensitive to the sun. Everyone's experience is different.

                                Your oncology team knows your plan. Please check with them before starting any new product, and especially before using actives like retinoids or acids.

                                Many people find comfort in a fragrance-free cleanser, a thick fragrance-free moisturizer, lukewarm water and soft towels.

                                Sun protection may matter more than usual during cancer treatment — ask your team what they recommend for you.

                                If your skin is painful, broken or changing quickly, let your care team know. You don't have to manage it alone.

                                Reviewed by: pending dermatologist review

                                General education, not medical advice. If something stings, burns or worries you, pause it and check with a dermatologist or your care team.

                                Basics

                                How to patch test

                                About a week of testing on a small area can save your whole face a rough week.

                                Pick a small spot: the inside of your elbow, or just below your jawline for face products.

                                Apply a small amount of the new product twice a day for 7 to 10 days. Some reactions take several days to show up, so it's worth the wait.

                                Watch for redness, itching, bumps or stinging. If you notice any, stop and give your skin a rest.

                                No reaction? Start using it on your face a few times a week before going daily. A patch test lowers the odds of a reaction; it can't rule one out completely.

                                If you have very reactive skin or a history of allergies, a dermatologist can help with more formal testing.

                                Reviewed by: pending dermatologist review

                                General education, not medical advice. If something stings, burns or worries you, pause it and check with a dermatologist or your care team.

                                Basics

                                Introduce one new product at a time

                                Adding one thing at a time is how you learn what your skin actually likes.

                                If you start three products in the same week and your skin reacts, there's no way to tell which one it was.

                                Add one new product, then wait about two weeks before adding the next. Actives often need longer — four to eight weeks — to show what they can do.

                                Log a quick check-in on most days while you try something new. SkinOracle notes when a product was opened, so patterns in your own logs can surface over time.

                                If your skin is irritated, go back to your simplest routine until it settles, then try again more slowly.

                                Reviewed by: pending dermatologist review

                                General education, not medical advice. If something stings, burns or worries you, pause it and check with a dermatologist or your care team.

                                Basics

                                Razor bumps

                                Small, itchy bumps after shaving are common for anyone with facial hair. A few shaving habits can help.

                                Razor bumps are small, sometimes itchy or tender bumps that can show up after shaving. They're common for anyone who shaves facial hair, and more common with curly or coarse hair.

                                Use a sharp single-blade or electric razor, and shave in the direction your hair grows. Rinse the blade after each stroke.

                                Don't pull your skin tight while you shave, and skip going over the same spot again and again.

                                Afterwards, a fragrance-free moisturizer or balm keeps skin comfortable. Letting your beard grow out for a while can also give skin a break.

                                If bumps keep coming back, hurt, or leave dark marks or scars, see a dermatologist. They can help you find an approach that works for your skin.

                                Reviewed by: pending dermatologist review

                                General education, not medical advice. If something stings, burns or worries you, pause it and check with a dermatologist or your care team.

                                Basics

                                Dark marks after spots

                                Marks left behind after a breakout usually fade with time. Daily sunscreen and patience help most.

                                After a spot or irritation settles, it can leave a flat brown, grey or dark mark. This is very common on medium and deeper skin tones, and dermatologists call it post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation.

                                These marks usually fade on their own, but it often takes months. Being gentle with your skin, and not picking, gives them the best chance.

                                Sun makes dark marks last longer. A broad-spectrum SPF every morning, whatever the weather, is the most helpful step. If you also notice larger dark patches, a tinted sunscreen with iron oxides adds protection from visible light.

                                Gentle ingredients like niacinamide or azelaic acid may help marks fade over time. Strong or several actives at once can irritate skin and leave new marks, so go slowly.

                                If marks aren't fading, are spreading, or you'd like more help, a dermatologist can talk you through options, especially for deeper skin tones.

                                Reviewed by: pending dermatologist review

                                General education, not medical advice. If something stings, burns or worries you, pause it and check with a dermatologist or your care team.
